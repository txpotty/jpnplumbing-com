    Tankless vs. Tank Water Heaters: What Works Best in Rhode Island

    By Travis Baines · September 18, 2026 · 6 min read

    Two Ways to Heat Water

    A tank heater keeps 40 to 80 gallons hot around the clock. A tankless unit heats water only while a tap is open. Both can be gas or electric, both can serve a whole house, and the choice usually comes down to how your family uses hot water and what your gas line and water will support. There is no single winner.

    The Tank Heater

    Tanks are simple and cheap to replace. A standard 50-gallon gas tank swap is usually a same-day job. The tradeoff is standby loss, since the burner or elements cycle all day to keep stored water hot whether anyone is home or not, and the tank holds a fixed amount, so the fourth shower in a row runs cold. Expect 8 to 12 years from a typical tank.

    Tanks also forgive a lot. A small gas line or some scale buildup rarely bothers them. If the power goes out, a gas tank with a standing pilot keeps making hot water, and the stored 50 gallons can even serve as an emergency supply drawn from the drain valve. That matters when the grid is down for a few days.

    Rhode Island has a large share of older housing, including multifamily homes with galvanized supply lines and cast iron drains.

    The Tankless Heater

    Tankless heaters are rated by flow, in gallons per minute at a given temperature rise. A large gas unit might deliver 8 or 9 gallons per minute when incoming water is mild. Colder incoming water means a bigger temperature rise, so the same unit delivers less flow in winter, and two showers plus a dishwasher can push a small unit past what it can keep up with. Get the size right first.

    The upside is real. You get continuous hot water, provided the unit was sized for the busiest hour in your house. Without standby loss, a gas tankless unit typically burns noticeably less fuel than a tank, it hangs on a wall instead of eating closet floor space, and a well-kept unit can run 20 years. The heat exchanger is the part to protect.

    Maintenance looks different on each. Drain a few gallons from a tank every year to pull out the sediment. The anode rod, a sacrificial magnesium or aluminum rod that keeps the steel from rusting, should be checked every three to five years, and replacing a spent anode is one of the cheapest ways to get extra years out of a tank. On a tankless unit, the yearly job is descaling plus cleaning the inlet filter.

    What Changes the Math in a Rhode Island Home

    Blue-green stains on sinks and tubs are a frequent sign of corrosive water, and treatment can protect the plumbing.

    Installation is where tankless costs climb. Expect a larger gas line, often 3/4-inch, plus new venting rated for the unit. Electric tankless models for a whole house can need 120 amps or more on dedicated breakers, which many older panels simply do not have room for, so a panel upgrade sometimes rides along with the job. Get that checked before you buy.

    Hard water is the other big variable. Scale coats a tankless heat exchanger quickly and trips error codes. On hard water, plan to descale the unit every year with a small pump and a descaling solution circulated through the isolation valves, or put a softener ahead of it. Tanks suffer from scale too, just more quietly.

    Salt air near the coast can rust exposed metal fixtures, outdoor spigots and fittings faster than inland.

    Family size and habits decide the rest. A couple in a small house rarely runs two showers at once. A family of five with teenagers and a soaking tub is a different load entirely, and that house needs either a large tankless unit sized for three fixtures at once or a bigger tank with a fast recovery rate. We ask how many showers run at the same time.

    Code details matter on either type. Every tank gets a T&P relief valve with a discharge line run down to within a few inches of the floor or to a drain. Gas units of both kinds need proper combustion air and venting, and a tankless unit needs isolation valves so it can be flushed without cutting into the plumbing every year. Inspectors check every one of those.

    Ask about recovery rate when comparing tanks. Gas tanks recover a lot quicker than electric tanks of the same size. First-hour rating, printed on the yellow energy label, tells you how many gallons a tank delivers in the first hour of heavy use, and it is a better number to compare than tank size alone for a busy house. Heat pump water heaters sit in between, with lower running costs and slower recovery.

    When to Call a Professional

    A replacement is the cheapest time to switch. A tank past 10 years or weeping at the base is worth pricing both ways now. Both options need a permit in most places.
