    Hydro Jetting vs. Snaking: Which Drain Cleaning Method Do You Need?

    By Aaron Mitchell · August 22, 2026 · 6 min read

    Know the Clog First

    Not every clog is the same, and the tool should match what is actually in the pipe. A wad of hair in a tub drain is a different job from grease coating 60 feet of kitchen line. Clogs fall into two groups, a single plug at one spot or buildup along the whole pipe wall, and figuring out which one you have is the first thing a good tech does. It starts with a couple of questions.

    Scale is the slow version of buildup. Old cast iron drains collect scale and rust that close the opening a little every year. A 4-inch cast iron line with heavy scale might have the flow area of a 2-inch pipe, which is why it clogs again a week after a cable goes through it. A snake punches through without removing it.

    Water in Rhode Island is generally soft and can be slightly acidic, which may cause pinhole leaks in copper pipe over time.

    How a Drain Snake Works

    A snake, or drain auger, is a coiled steel cable spun by a hand crank or a motor. On the end is a cutter or a corkscrew spring that drills into the blockage and breaks it apart or grabs it. Hand augers handle sinks and tubs, a closet auger handles toilets, a small drum machine handles laundry and kitchen lines, and a sectional machine with a 3/4-inch cable reaches a main line out to 100 feet or more. It is fast and cheap.

    Snaking is the right first call for a single hard blockage. A toilet stopped by a toy or a main plugged by roots at one joint both clear well with a cable. The weakness is that a cable opens a hole roughly the size of its cutter, so grease and scale on the rest of the pipe wall stay right where they were. A clog that returns within weeks is the giveaway.

    Cable size matters as much as the machine. Small hand cables suit lavatory drains, not main sewer lines. Running too big a cable through a small trap can crack it, and running a small cable through a main just pokes a hole through a root mass that grows back within weeks. Matching the two takes experience.

    How Hydro Jetting Works

    A jetter pushes water through a hose at roughly 3,000 to 4,000 psi for residential lines. Its nozzle fires one jet forward to break through and a ring of rear jets that drive the hose and scrub the pipe. As the tech reels the hose back, the rear jets strip grease, scale, sludge and roots off the pipe all the way around, and the debris flushes downstream to the sewer. The pipe comes out close to bare.

    Jetting shines on greasy kitchen lines and scaled cast iron. Restaurants have their lines jetted on a schedule for exactly this reason. It also clears sand and silt that a cable just pushes around, and a jetted line usually stays clear far longer than a cabled one, often years instead of months on a grease problem. It costs more per visit.

    Jetting needs a way in. Most jobs run through an outdoor cleanout, which keeps the water and debris outside the house. Homes without one may need a cleanout installed first, usually a quick job with a fitting and a riser at the foundation, and that new access point pays for itself the next time the line needs work. Pulling a toilet works in a pinch.

    Choosing Between Them

    Jetting is not safe for every pipe. High pressure can blow through cast iron that is paper thin or clay that is already broken. That is why a good tech runs a camera first on an older line, both to see what the clog is and to check whether the pipe can take a jetter without making the problem worse. Skip that step and a cleaning can turn into a dig.

    Here is the rough rule we use. One hard plug in pipe that is in decent shape gets snaked. A kitchen line that clogs every few months or a main coated with grease or scale gets a camera and then a jetter, and that combination usually stops the repeat calls for a long time. Cost plays into it too.

    When to Call a Professional

    Rental drum machines are where a lot of homeowner drain jobs go sideways. A cable that kinks inside the pipe can get stuck or whip around and hurt someone. If a plunger and a small hand auger have not cleared it, or more than one drain is backing up, call JPN Plumbing at (401) 364-2507 and describe which fixtures are affected, because that tells the tech which line to start on and which tools to bring. Mention when the last cleaning was.
