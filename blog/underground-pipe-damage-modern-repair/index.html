    Underground Pipe Damage: Common Causes and Modern Repair Solutions

    By Luis Ortega · August 31, 2026 · 5 min read

    What Goes Wrong Under the Yard

    Your sewer lateral and water service run from the house to the street, usually somewhere 2 to 6 feet underground. They stay out of mind until the lawn gets soggy or a toilet backs up. A typical lateral is 4-inch pipe, and older homes often have clay tile or cast iron. Each fails in its own way.

    Common Causes

    Tree Roots

    Roots find sewer lines by following moisture from a cracked joint or a loose gasket. Once a hair-thin root gets in, it grows into a mat that catches paper and grease. Clay pipe is the worst for this. Its joints come every couple of feet, and a big maple or oak can pack a 4-inch line full in a few growing seasons.

    Shifting Soil

    Basements are standard in most homes, and many rely on sump pumps to manage groundwater during wet seasons.

    Soil that settles or swells moves the pipe with it. Rigid pipe like clay or cast iron does not bend, so it either cracks or separates at a joint. Sags are common too. A sag, which we call a belly, holds water and waste, and on camera it looks like a stretch that stays full after flushing.

    Old Pipe Material

    Rhode Island has a large share of older housing, including multifamily homes with galvanized supply lines and cast iron drains.

    Age wears every material down. Cast iron rusts from the inside, and the bottom of the pipe can corrode through while the top still looks solid. Orangeburg, which builders used into the early 1970s, is tar-soaked fiber pipe that slowly squashes flat. Old galvanized water lines rust shut inside and drop the pressure everywhere.

    Signs of a Buried Line Problem

    Several drains slowing at once points to the main line. One classic sign is a toilet that gurgles as the washer empties. Outside, look for a patch of grass that stays greener than the rest, or soft ground over the path of the line, especially in dry weather. Sewer smell near the cleanout is another.

    On the water side, the meter tells the story. Turn off every fixture and watch the leak indicator, the small triangle or dial on the meter face. When that indicator spins, water is escaping between the meter and the house, often from the buried service. Low pressure throughout the house can mean the same.

    A sewer camera settles it. A camera goes in at the cleanout and records the line, and a locator marks the depth and location on the surface. We show you the footage as it plays. The footage decides the repair.

    Modern Repair Options

    Trench repair is still the right call for some jobs. Collapsed pipe or a badly sagging run generally gets excavated, because a liner needs pipe that still holds its shape. Spot repairs can be small. One cracked joint might only need a four-by-six hole and a day on site.

    Trenchless lining avoids most of the digging. A felt liner soaked in epoxy resin is pulled into the line, inflated, and cured into a pipe within the pipe. Most residential liners go in through the cleanout and one small access pit, and the job takes a day. Roots cannot get through it.

    Pipe bursting is the other trenchless method. A cone-shaped head is winched through the old pipe, splitting it and pulling new HDPE pipe into place behind it. You need a pit on each end, but the lawn in between stays untouched. Our crews at JPN Plumbing use both methods across Rhode Island, depending on what the camera shows.

    When to Call

    Do not wait on a sewer line that has backed up twice. Every backup leaves more waste behind, and that feeds the roots. A typical camera inspection takes less than an hour, and we hand you the video along with the marked location of the damage. Reach JPN Plumbing at (401) 364-2507. It helps if you know where your cleanout is, because that saves time on the visit.
